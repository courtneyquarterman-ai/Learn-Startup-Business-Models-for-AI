Learner Explanation: Quantum BLOOM Breast Cancer Classification

1. The workflow

I wanted to know whether a quantum kernel could classify breast tumors as well as a standard classical model. I used the Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset: 569 tumors, each with 30 measurements and a benign or malignant label.

First I held back 20% of the data (114 tumors) as a test set the models never see during training. From the remaining 455, I took a stratified sample of 120 for training, meaning it keeps the same benign-to-malignant mix as the full data. I used seed 42 so anyone can get the same split.

Next I prepared the data. I standardized the 30 features and used PCA to shrink them to 4 components, which keep about 81% of the variation in the data. I did this because a 4-qubit circuit can only take 4 inputs. Then I rescaled those 4 values to angles between 0 and pi so the circuit could use them. I fit all of these steps on the training data only. Fitting them on the test data would leak information and make the results look better than they really are.

The quantum part is the kernel. A ZZ feature map (2 repetitions, linear entanglement) turns each tumor into a quantum state. The kernel score for two tumors is how much their states overlap, from 0 (completely different) to 1 (identical). I calculated these exactly with statevector simulation. As a check, I also ran one pair through 2,048 measurement shots: the estimate was 0.178, close to the exact 0.175. The SVM itself is still trained classically. It just uses the quantum similarity scores in place of a classical kernel.

2. The classical comparison

I compared three models on the same 120 training tumors and the same 114 test tumors:

- RBF SVM with all 30 features: 96.5% accuracy, caught 39 of 42 malignant tumors
- RBF SVM with the same 4 PCA inputs: 93.0% accuracy, caught 35 of 42
- Quantum ZZ SVM with the same 4 PCA inputs: 64.0% accuracy, caught 12 of 42

The first comparison shows what PCA costs. Going from 30 features to 4 dropped accuracy only a little. The second comparison is the fair head-to-head: same inputs, different kernel. The classical kernel clearly won.

In this setting, the number that matters most is missed malignant tumors. The quantum model missed 30 of 42 and also flagged 11 benign tumors as malignant. Its ROC-AUC of 0.71 means it ranked tumors somewhat better than chance, but at the default decision threshold it mostly predicted benign. In this experiment, quantum did not beat classical, and I think saying so plainly is the honest result.

3. Limitations

- This is one small training set and one test split, so it doesn't show how these models perform in general.
- Every model used the same untuned starting settings (C = 1). That makes the comparison fair, but it's not a contest between fully optimized models.
- I've already seen the test scores, so if I tune the models now, I'd need fresh data or a new evaluation plan to make any honest claims.
- Everything ran in simulation. The shot test adds sampling variation but not real hardware noise, and the timings say nothing about quantum speed on a real device.
- One possible reason the quantum model did poorly: most similarity scores between training tumors were very low, so the kernel made almost every tumor look different from every other. Adjusting the angle scale could test this. That's a hypothesis, not a finding.
- This is a classroom dataset, not a clinical tool.
- My notebook shows what I ran, but it doesn't prove I understand it. That's why a reviewer should ask me follow-up questions.
